The web, except nobody can quietly change the page you are reading.

Normally a page lives at an address like example.com/about, and whoever runs that address can swap the contents whenever they like. Here, a page's address is a fingerprint of the page itself. Change one letter, and the address no longer matches — so what you read is what was published.

1

Open a page that lives at its own fingerprint

This example site is two files — a page and its stylesheet. Each has its own fingerprint, and the site as a whole has one too. Nobody is hosting it in the usual sense; it is fetched by what it is.

2

Share something of your own

Write a note and publish it. You get back a link that works for anyone who has this demo running — and that link is tied to your exact words. Edit a word later and it becomes a different link, while the old one keeps showing the original.

3

See how a takedown actually works

A gateway is a public service, so it has to answer for what it hands out. Below is a stand-in page. Report it, and the gateway stops serving it — the page is not deleted, it just stops being available from here. That is the whole mechanism, and it is worth seeing once.

What is actually going on here

Every piece of content is hashed — boiled down to a short fingerprint — and that fingerprint becomes its address. Ask a gateway for an address and it fetches the content, recomputes the fingerprint, and refuses to serve anything that does not match. So the gateway cannot lie to you about what a page says, and a takedown can only ever mean "this gateway declines to serve it", never "this content no longer exists".

This demo runs entirely on your own machine: the pages are stored in a folder next to the program, and there is no network of other computers involved. The fingerprints, the verification and the takedown behaviour are real; the peer-to-peer part is not.
